Our evaluation demonstrates \sys{}'s effectiveness across four LLM model families with sizes ranging from 7B--70B: Vicuna, Mistral, Llama, and Qwen~\cite{vicuna, jiang2023mistral7b, touvron2023llama, qwen}. We experiment on three hardware platforms with PCIe and NVLink interconnects and across tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism, focusing first on tensor parallelism, which presents the most communication-intensive prediction setting.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{ss:esetup}

We conducted experiments on three hardware platforms: (1) NVIDIA RTX A6000; (2) NVIDIA B40; and (3) NVIDIA H200. Platform (1) uses an AMD EPYC Milan 7543P 32-core CPU with four A6000 GPUs over PCIe 4.0. Platforms (2) and (3) use an Intel Xeon 6 6530P 32-core CPU, with PCIe 4.0 for B40 and NVLink-connected GPUs for H200. Ground-truth system power/energy is measured using an external Watts Up Pro monitor on platform~1 and IPMI/BMC power readings on platforms~2--3.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Training methodology.}
We train module-level predictors using 3-fold cross-validation, with 70\% of datapoints used for training and 30\% for testing in each split. Each module type (e.g., Self-Attention, MLP, AllReduce) is sampled $10{,}000$ times; each sample is an aggregate measurement obtained by repeatedly executing the module under a fixed GPU configuration. For each sample, we aggregate multi-GPU runtime features using their mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, and combine these with the structural features in Table~\ref{tab:features}. We sample batch sizes 8, 16, 32, and 64 and output sequence lengths 512 and 1024 to cover a range of inference loads. All parallel inference experiments use DeepSpeed~\cite{deepspeed}.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare \sys{} against four baselines. \textbf{IrEne}~\cite{irene} is the closest methodological baseline: it uses a hierarchical model-tree predictor for single-GPU execution but does not explicitly model inter-GPU communication. \textbf{CodeCarbon}~\cite{codecarbon} is a software-based energy/$\mathrm{CO_2}$ estimator that integrates telemetry-derived power over time. \textbf{Wilkins et al.}~\cite{wilkins2024offlineenergyoptimalllmserving} estimate per-request energy from input/output token counts; we fit their form $e_K(\tau_{\mathrm{in}},\tau_{\mathrm{out}})=\alpha_0\tau_{\mathrm{in}}+\alpha_1\tau_{\mathrm{out}}+\alpha_2\tau_{\mathrm{in}}\tau_{\mathrm{out}}$ on a calibration set. \textbf{NVML}~\cite{nvml} provides GPU-only power/energy measurements; we train a model-level linear regressor using NVML energy to predict measured total-system energy. We omit the software estimator of Strubell et al.~\cite{strubell19} because it is outperformed by stronger baselines such as IrEne.

\subsection{Energy Prediction for Tensor Parallelism}
\label{sec:model_prediction}

Figures~\ref{fig:mape_comparison} and~\ref{fig:b40_mape_tensor_parallelism} show mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for \sys{} and the baselines under tensor parallelism on A6000 and B40 GPUs, respectively. For each model family, predictors are trained across model variants, batch sizes, and sequence lengths using the methodology in \S\ref{ss:esetup}.

We see that \be{\sys{} consistently achieves the lowest MAPE in all cases} on the A6000 platform, with an average of 17.6\%. By contrast, CodeCarbon incurs an average MAPE of 28.49\%, about 1.7$\times$ that of \sys{}. Its reliance on coarse GPU profiling and slow sampling misses the energy use of fine-grained multi-GPU communication events. IrEne incurs 40.45\% average MAPE (about 3$\times$ that of \sys{}) because it ignores communication energy, while Wilkins et al. incurs 58.77\% average MAPE because its token-in/token-out regression does not model inter-GPU communication.

The B40 results in Figure~\ref{fig:b40_mape_tensor_parallelism} show similar trends, with \sys{} achieving an average MAPE of 15.5\%. The gap between \sys{} and the baselines generally widens with increasing parallelization because the AllReduce contribution increases with GPU count (Figure~\ref{fig:all_reduce_energy}). We also evaluated \sys{} on 2$\times$H200 GPUs across all twelve model variants: model-level MAPE ranges from 13.34\% to 26.53\%, with an average of 16.8\%. Module-level H200 errors remain in the single-digit range (7.44--9.25\%), providing additional evidence that the same decomposition and communication model transfer to a third GPU platform.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{NVML baseline.}
Using NVML-reported GPU energy as the sole predictor for total-system energy produces 29.8--44.2\% MAPE on A6000 across the evaluated models. Its generalization is worse: under leave-one-variant-out evaluation, NVML-based regression yields 47.4--57.3\% MAPE (51.5\% on average), compared with 15.8--24.5\% for \sys{}. We observe similarly high NVML errors on B40, confirming that GPU-only energy is an insufficient proxy for total-system energy in tensor-parallel settings.

Data parallel inference incurs less frequent and more structured communication, so the penalty for omitting communication is smaller than for pipeline parallelism, though still substantially higher than \sys{}.

\subsection{Ablation Study: Communication Model}
\label{sec:ablation_comm_modeling}

To isolate the benefit of explicitly modeling communication energy (\S\ref{ss:design-communication}), we evaluate an ablated version of \sys{} under tensor parallelism that retains the model-tree decomposition and compute-module predictors but replaces our communication model with a simpler estimate: average observed AllReduce power multiplied by the duration of each AllReduce call. We denote this variant \textsc{AvgComm}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/mape_all_model_families_no_comm_modeling.pdf}
    \vspace{-24pt}
    \caption{Ablation of explicit communication modeling. The ablated variant replaces \sys{}'s communication model with an average communication-power estimate for AllReduce events.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_comm_modeling} shows that \textsc{AvgComm} incurs substantially higher prediction error of 34.0\% compared with \sys{}'s 12.9\%. The increase is especially noticeable for larger models and 4-GPU settings, where AllReduce contributes a larger fraction of total energy. The key distinction is that \sys{} models communication as a first-class module whose features reflect the actual communication, whereas \textsc{AvgComm} collapses different transfer sizes and communication behavior into a single average value.

\subsection{Ablation Study: Model Tree Methodology}
\label{sec:ablation}

To isolate the importance of \sys{}'s model-tree abstraction, we evaluate a direct model-level prediction ablation that removes module-level decomposition and trains a single regressor to predict model energy from aggregate model-level features. Direct prediction produces substantially higher MAPE: 61.2\% for Vicuna, 84.3\% for Mistral, 115.1\% for Llama, and 60.8\% for Qwen. This shows that parallel-inference energy is not simply a model-level regression problem: compute-heavy modules (e.g., Attention and MLP) and communication-heavy modules (e.g., AllReduce) respond differently to runtime and architectural features, and collapsing them into one aggregate signal loses this structure.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Latency-style prediction ablation.}
We additionally test whether a recent latency-prediction methodology can be repurposed directly for energy. Following NeuSight~\cite{neusight}, we retain its fine-grained decomposition and MLP predictor but replace latency with measured system energy as the target. This produces MAPE of 79.19\%, 93.24\%, and 89.38\% for Vicuna-7B, 13B, and 33B, respectively (87.3\% average), roughly 3--5$\times$ worse than \sys{}. Thus, latency-oriented execution features alone do not capture the power dynamics, communication overheads, and synchronization effects that determine parallel-inference energy.

\subsection{Use case: Inference Time vs. Energy for Different Levels of Tensor Parallelization}
\label{sec:usecase}

We now present a use case to highlight the applicability of \sys{} in practice. Consider a developer choosing both an LLM and the number of GPUs across which to deploy it, while seeking to reduce inference time and energy consumption. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_plot} illustrates the trade-off between predicted energy and inference time for Vicuna-7B, 13B, and 33B across 1-, 2-, and 4-GPU tensor-parallel configurations. We use the highest batch size achievable for each model size; results are normalized per token and energy is shown on a log scale. Compared with ground truth, prediction errors are around 15\%.

Both per-token energy and inference time increase with model size, but decrease as the degree of parallelization increases. For Vicuna-7B, increasing GPU count from 2 to 4 reduces energy by 43\% but improves inference time by only 20\%, showing diminishing returns. In contrast, Vicuna-33B sees a 33\% energy reduction and a 50\% speed improvement. Qwen exhibits a different scaling profile: Qwen-32B achieves a 40\% energy reduction from 2 to 4 GPUs, compared with 33\% for Vicuna-33B, while maintaining similar speedups.

The energy impact of parallelization is therefore not obvious from model size or GPU count alone. Without accurate predictions, developers must perform numerous trial-and-error experiments to make energy-conscious parallelization decisions; \sys{} reduces this effort by providing scalable energy predictions across configurations.
